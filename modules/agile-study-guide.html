What is Agile?

Agile is a mindset, philosophy, and set of practices for software development that prioritizes flexibility, collaboration, customer feedback, and rapid delivery of working software over rigid planning and documentation. It is not a single methodology — it is a family of frameworks united by shared values.

The core idea: Instead of spending months planning everything upfront then building in one shot, Agile teams deliver working software in small, frequent increments (called iterations or sprints), continuously incorporating customer feedback and adapting to change.

Agile is NOT a methodology

This is the most common misconception. Agile is a philosophy and mindset described by 4 values and 12 principles in the Agile Manifesto. The actual methodologies (Scrum, Kanban, XP, SAFe, etc.) are implementations of the Agile mindset.

The beliefs: flexibility over rigidity, collaboration over silos, working software over documentation, customer partnership over contracts.

Why Agile exists — The problem it solves

The Standish CHAOS Report: Studies consistently show that 30–40% of software projects fail outright and another 40–50% are significantly challenged (over budget, late, missing features). Traditional "big design upfront" approaches couldn't handle changing requirements.
❌ The Traditional Problem
Requirements change mid-project. Markets shift. Customers realize what they actually wanted only after seeing the first version. A 12-month plan becomes obsolete within 3 months. The team builds the wrong thing — perfectly.
✅ The Agile Solution
Deliver a small working piece every 2 weeks. Show it to customers. Get feedback. Adjust. Repeat. By month 3, you've already delivered value and course-corrected three times. Failure is discovered early and cheaply.

Key Agile concepts you must know

Iteration / Sprint
A fixed time box (usually 1–4 weeks) during which the team builds and delivers a potentially shippable increment of the product.
Increment
A tangible, working, tested piece of software delivered at the end of each iteration. Must be potentially releasable to users.
Backlog
A prioritized list of everything the product needs. The team pulls work from the top. New items can be added anytime — the backlog evolves.
Velocity
How much work a team completes per sprint (measured in story points or hours). Used for forecasting, not performance management.
Definition of Done
The shared checklist that must be met for any piece of work to be called "done." Prevents half-finished work from being counted as complete.
Retrospective
A team meeting at the end of each sprint to reflect on the process and identify one or two improvements for the next sprint.
Module 2 · Context

History & Timeline

Agile did not appear out of thin air. It evolved from decades of frustration with heavyweight software processes and multiple earlier lightweight methodologies. Understanding the history helps you understand why Agile is designed the way it is.

1970s–1980s
The Waterfall Era
Winston Royce's 1970 paper described a sequential development process (later named "Waterfall"). IBM and DoD mandated heavyweight processes. CMMI, ISO 9001 defined rigorous, documentation-heavy standards. Works for known, stable requirements — terrible for software.
1986
Scrum origins — "The New New Product Development Game"
Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka publish a Harvard Business Review paper comparing the most productive development teams to rugby teams. They coin the term "Scrum" from rugby. The paper describes overlapping, cross-functional, self-organizing teams producing breakthroughs.
1991–1995
Lightweight methods emerge
RAD (Rapid Application Development) by James Martin (1991), DSDM founded in UK (1994), Scrum formalized by Jeff Sutherland & Ken Schwaber (1995) at OOPSLA conference, Feature-Driven Development (FDD) by Jeff De Luca, Crystal by Alistair Cockburn at IBM.
1999
Extreme Programming (XP) — Kent Beck
Kent Beck publishes "Extreme Programming Explained." XP introduces pair programming, test-driven development (TDD), continuous integration, refactoring, and the concept of the "planning game." Heavily technical and engineering-focused.
February 2001 ⭐
The Agile Manifesto — Snowbird, Utah
17 software practitioners (including Kent Beck, Jeff Sutherland, Ken Schwaber, Martin Fowler, Bob Martin) meet at a ski resort and write the Agile Manifesto in two days. Four values, 12 principles. The document that names and unites the movement. Still the foundation today.
2001–2010
Agile adoption accelerates
Scrum becomes dominant. The Scrum Guide published by Sutherland & Schwaber. Kanban for software introduced by David Anderson (2007). VersionOne begins tracking Agile adoption (2006). By 2010, Agile is mainstream in software.
2011–2018
Scaling Agile — SAFe, LeSS, Nexus
Companies need Agile for hundreds of teams. SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework) by Dean Leffingwell (2011), LeSS by Craig Larman & Bas Vodde, Nexus by Ken Schwaber. Enterprise Agile transformation becomes a billion-dollar consulting industry.
2019–Present
Agile matures, critiques emerge
Debate about "Dark Agile" / "Agile theater" — cargo-culting ceremonies without the mindset. "The Death of Agile" discourse. 2023 State of Agile report: 71% of organizations use Agile. DevOps and Platform Engineering reshape how Agile is practiced. Focus shifts to outcomes over process.
Module 3 · Foundation

The Agile Manifesto

Written in February 2001 by 17 software practitioners in Snowbird, Utah. Four values that changed software development forever. Every Agile framework derives from these four statements.

The full opening: "We are uncovering better ways of developing software by doing it and helping others do it. Through this work we have come to value…"

The Four Core Values

1
Individuals and interactions over processes and tools
People and how they communicate are more important than the tools they use or the processes they follow. A great team with mediocre tools outperforms a mediocre team with great tools every time. This doesn't mean processes and tools are worthless — it means human communication and collaboration come first.

In practice: Face-to-face conversations over email threads. Team autonomy over strict role definitions. Trust over surveillance. The best architectures emerge from self-organizing teams.
2
Working software over comprehensive documentation
The primary measure of progress is working software, not documentation. Agile doesn't reject documentation — it questions documentation that consumes energy without delivering value. A 200-page spec document that nobody reads is less valuable than a working feature.

In practice: Ship working features every sprint. Write just enough documentation to be useful. Use executable tests as living documentation. Code that works is proof — documents that say it works are not.
3
Customer collaboration over contract negotiation
Work with customers continuously rather than negotiate rigid contracts upfront and only deliver at the end. Contracts create adversarial relationships. Collaboration creates partnership. When requirements change (and they always do), a collaborative relationship allows graceful adaptation.

In practice: Product owners embedded in the team. Regular sprint reviews with stakeholders. Continuous feedback loops. Embrace change requests rather than fight them through change control boards.
4
Responding to change over following a plan
Adaptability is more valuable than adherence to a plan. In a complex, fast-moving environment, a plan made 6 months ago is likely wrong. Agile teams plan continuously in short horizons rather than once in long horizons. This doesn't mean planning is bad — it means plans must remain flexible.

In practice: Reprioritize the backlog each sprint. Accept mid-sprint feedback. Plan the sprint, adapt during execution. "No plan survives first contact with the enemy" — Agile is designed for reality, not theory.
Critical nuance: The manifesto explicitly says "while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more." This means Agile does NOT eliminate plans, documentation, tools, or contracts. It prioritizes the left-hand values when they conflict with the right.

The 12 Agile Principles

Behind the four values are 12 principles that give detailed guidance on how to practice Agile. Every Agile ceremony, artifact, and practice traces back to one or more of these principles. Know these deeply.

Principle 1 — Customer satisfaction through early and continuous delivery +
"Our highest priority is to satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery of valuable software."

What it means: Don't wait months to show customers something. Ship working features early — even if small — so customers can verify you're building the right thing. Continuous delivery means every sprint produces something a customer could potentially use.

In Scrum: Sprint Reviews show working software every 2 weeks. The increment must be potentially shippable. In Kanban: work flows continuously to done.
Principle 2 — Welcome changing requirements, even late in development +
"Welcome changing requirements, even late in development. Agile processes harness change for the customer's competitive advantage."

What it means: Change is not a problem to be controlled — it's information that helps you build better products. A competitor launches a new feature; a customer's market shifts; technical discoveries change the approach. Agile teams can respond in the next sprint.

Anti-pattern: "We can't add that — it's not in scope." Agile teams reprioritize the backlog instead of refusing change.
Principle 3 — Deliver working software frequently (weeks, not months) +
"Deliver working software frequently, from a couple of weeks to a couple of months, with a preference to the shorter timescale."

What it means: Short delivery cycles reduce risk, create feedback loops, and keep teams focused. The shorter the cycle, the faster you discover mistakes. 2-week sprints are the most common implementation. Continuous deployment (CD) takes this to the extreme: deploy every commit.

Why it matters: A 6-month release cycle means 6 months of accumulated risk. A 2-week sprint cycle means 2 weeks of accumulated risk.
Principle 4 — Business and developers must work together daily +
"Business people and developers must work together daily throughout the project."

What it means: The wall between "IT" and "business" is an Agile anti-pattern. Product Owners, stakeholders, and customers must be accessible — not hidden behind requirements docs. Daily collaboration eliminates the "telephone game" of requirements passing through layers of intermediaries.

In practice: The Product Owner is a full-time team member. Developers can directly ask the PO questions. Stakeholders attend Sprint Reviews and give feedback directly.
Principle 5 — Build projects around motivated individuals with trust and support +
"Build projects around motivated individuals. Give them the environment and support they need, and trust them to get the job done."

What it means: Hire talented, motivated people. Remove obstacles. Provide tools and environment. Then get out of their way. Micromanagement and Agile are fundamentally incompatible. Command-and-control management destroys the self-organization that Agile requires.

Contrast: Traditional management: assign tasks, monitor hours, approve decisions. Agile management: set goals, provide context, remove blockers, trust the team.
Principle 6 — Face-to-face conversation is the most effective communication +
"The most efficient and effective method of conveying information to and within a development team is face-to-face conversation."

What it means: Synchronous, in-person (or video) conversation conveys context, tone, body language, and immediate feedback that written messages cannot. A 5-minute conversation resolves what 20 email exchanges cannot.

Remote teams: This principle drives the move to video calls, virtual whiteboards, and synchronous collaboration in remote Agile teams. It doesn't mean everyone must be co-located — it means prioritize rich communication channels.
Principle 7 — Working software is the primary measure of progress +
"Working software is the primary measure of progress."

What it means: Not lines of code. Not hours logged. Not percentage complete. Not documentation delivered. The only true measure of progress is working, tested, potentially shippable software. This protects against "90% done" projects that stay 90% done forever.

Anti-pattern: Reporting "50 story points burned" without verifying the features actually work. Definition of Done must include tested and demo-able.
Principle 8 — Agile processes promote sustainable development (no crunch culture) +
"Agile processes promote sustainable development. The sponsors, developers, and users should be able to maintain a constant pace indefinitely."

What it means: Software is a marathon, not a sprint (ironically). Burnout, crunch culture, and hero culture are Agile anti-patterns. A team that works 80-hour weeks for 2 months becomes unproductive for the next 6. Sustainable velocity is higher than unsustainable bursts.

In practice: Teams push back on overloaded sprints. Sprint velocity naturally stabilizes. Overtime is a signal of a planning problem, not a badge of honor.
Principle 9 — Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design +
"Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design enhances agility."

What it means: Technical debt is the enemy of agility. A codebase full of shortcuts, no tests, and poor design becomes harder to change over time — exactly when Agile needs it to be easy to change. TDD, refactoring, clean code, and SOLID principles are Agile enablers, not optional extras.

Warning: "We'll refactor later" becomes "we can't change anything without breaking everything." Prioritize tech excellence from day one.
Principle 10 — Simplicity — maximizing the amount of work not done +
"Simplicity — the art of maximizing the amount of work not done — is essential."

What it means: YAGNI — "You Ain't Gonna Need It." Don't build features users didn't ask for. Don't add complexity that isn't needed now. The simplest solution that works is the best solution. Over-engineering is waste.

In practice: Ruthless backlog prioritization. Minimal viable features. Delete dead code. Say no to gold-plating. Every line of code you don't write is a line you never have to maintain.
Principle 11 — Self-organizing teams produce the best architectures +
"The best architectures, requirements, and designs emerge from self-organizing teams."

What it means: Don't have an architect who designs in isolation and hands specs to developers. The people doing the work have the best understanding of the problems and solutions. Self-organizing teams decide HOW to accomplish their goals — management sets the WHAT and WHY.

Contrast: In Waterfall, architects design → developers implement. In Agile, the team collectively owns architecture decisions. Emergent design through continuous refactoring and collective code ownership.
Principle 12 — Regular reflection and adaptation (retrospectives) +
"At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then tunes and adjusts its behavior accordingly."

What it means: This is the engine of continuous improvement. The Scrum retrospective is the direct implementation of this principle. Every sprint, the team asks: What went well? What didn't? What do we change? The team that improves its process consistently outperforms one that doesn't.

Anti-pattern: Skipping retrospectives when "we're too busy." The busy team needs retrospectives more than the un-busy one.

When Waterfall is actually better

Waterfall is not always wrong. It excels in environments with truly fixed, well-understood requirements, regulatory mandates requiring specific documentation, or physical-world constraints that make iteration impossible.
✅ Use Waterfall when…
Requirements are stable and well-understood (building a bridge, FDA drug approval). Regulatory compliance requires formal sign-offs at each stage (medical devices, aerospace, defense). Short-duration projects with a single, well-understood deliverable. Integration with fixed external systems with known APIs.
✅ Use Agile when…
Requirements are uncertain or likely to change. You need to deliver value before the full product is done. You're building software in a competitive market. Customer input during development is available and valuable. The team is small to medium and can self-organize.

Scrum — The Complete Guide

Scrum is the most widely used Agile framework. It provides a lightweight structure with three roles, five events, and three artifacts to deliver complex products in environments with changing requirements. Understanding Scrum is the foundation of Agile practice.

What Scrum actually is

Scrum is a framework — not a methodology. The 2020 Scrum Guide is only 13 pages long. It is intentionally lightweight and incomplete: it tells you the rules of the game, but leaves the specific plays to you. This is a design choice — Scrum provides structure without prescribing solutions.

The Scrum definition: "Scrum is a lightweight framework that helps people, teams and organizations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems." — Scrum Guide 2020.

The Three Pillars of Scrum (Empiricism)

Scrum is founded on empirical process control — knowledge comes from experience and decisions are made based on what is known. This requires three pillars:

🔍 Transparency
Everyone must have the same understanding of what's happening. The Backlog, Sprint Board, Definition of Done, and team progress are visible to all stakeholders. No hidden information.
🔬 Inspection
Scrum artifacts and progress toward goals must be inspected frequently. All five Scrum events are inspection points. Teams regularly check: Are we building the right thing? Are we on track?
🔧 Adaptation
If inspection reveals that something is unacceptable, the process or product must be adjusted. Sprint Retrospective is the adaptation event for process. Sprint Review adapts the product direction.
Scrum Deep Dive · Roles

Scrum Roles

Scrum defines exactly three roles (called "accountabilities" in the 2020 Guide). Each has specific responsibilities that do not overlap. Getting roles right is the foundation of a functional Scrum team.

The Scrum Team

A Scrum Team consists of one Scrum Master, one Product Owner, and Developers. It is a cohesive unit of professionals focused on one objective at a time — the Product Goal. The team is small enough to be nimble and large enough to complete significant work (typically 10 or fewer people including SM and PO).

Key principle: The Scrum Team is cross-functional (has all skills needed to create value each Sprint) and self-managing (the team decides who does what, when, and how — nobody outside the team tells the developers how to turn items into Increments).
👤
Product Owner (PO)
One person · Accountable for product value
Primary accountability: Maximizing the value of the product resulting from the Scrum Team's work. The PO is the single voice of the customer and business.

Specific responsibilities:
— Creates, maintains, and clearly communicates the Product Backlog
— Orders Product Backlog items (prioritization)
— Ensures the Product Backlog is transparent, visible, and understood
— Accepts or rejects Sprint output
— Defines the Product Goal
— The only person authorized to cancel a Sprint

Must be one person — not a committee. The PO may represent many stakeholders' needs, but they are a single accountable individual. Decisions are made by this one person.

Common failure modes: PO who is "part-time" (unavailable to team), PO who lets stakeholders bypass them to give developers direct orders, PO who doesn't say "no" to low-priority requests.
🧭
Scrum Master (SM)
One person · Servant-leader · Process coach
Primary accountability: Establishing Scrum as defined in the Scrum Guide and helping the team and organization understand and enact Scrum theory and practice. The SM is a servant-leader — serving the team, not managing it.

Serves the Scrum Team by:
— Coaching team members on self-management and cross-functionality
— Removing impediments to progress (blockers the team cannot remove themselves)
— Facilitating Scrum events (not running them — facilitating)
— Training team in creating clear Product Backlog items

Serves the Product Owner by:
— Helping find techniques for Product Goal definition and Backlog management
— Facilitating stakeholder collaboration

Serves the Organization by:
— Leading, training, and coaching Agile adoption
— Planning and advising Scrum implementations

What the SM is NOT: A project manager. A secretary who takes minutes. A team lead. A resource manager. A meeting organizer. A tracker of hours.
💻
Developers
3–9 people · Cross-functional · Self-organizing
Primary accountability: Creating any aspect of a usable Increment each Sprint. Despite the name "Developers," this includes everyone who contributes to building the product — software engineers, QA engineers, UX designers, data scientists, etc.

Specific responsibilities:
— Creating the Sprint Backlog (the plan for the Sprint)
— Adapting their plan each day toward the Sprint Goal
— Holding each other accountable as professionals
— Creating the Increment according to the Definition of Done

Key characteristic: The Developers have no sub-teams or hierarchies. No lead developer, no architect role in Scrum. All Developers are equal. They collectively own how work gets done.

Ideal size: 3–9 Developers. Too few = not enough skills. Too many = too much coordination overhead. If the team is larger, consider creating multiple Scrum Teams with one shared Product Backlog.
Scrum Deep Dive · Events

Scrum Events

Scrum prescribes five events (previously called "ceremonies"). All events are timeboxed — they have a maximum duration. Events create regularity and minimize the need for meetings not defined in Scrum.

Important: All Scrum events happen within and including the Sprint. The Sprint is the container for all other events. Skipping Scrum events is anti-Scrum and reduces transparency and the opportunity to inspect and adapt.
📦
The Sprint
1–4 weeks · Fixed timebox · The heartbeat of Scrum
The Sprint is the container event. All other events happen within a Sprint. Each Sprint is a short project with a goal, a plan, the execution, and a potentially releasable product.

Sprint rules:
— Fixed length. Never shortened or extended (except: only the PO can cancel a Sprint in extreme cases)
— No changes that endanger the Sprint Goal
— Quality does not decrease
— The Product Backlog is refined as needed
— Scope may be clarified and renegotiated with the PO as more is learned

Recommended length: 2 weeks for most software teams. 1 week for very urgent, fast-changing work. 4 weeks for hardware-dependent teams. Shorter = faster feedback = less risk.
🗓️
Sprint Planning
Max 8 hours per month · Start of each sprint · Whole team
The team plans the work to be done during the Sprint. The Scrum Team answers three questions:

Topic 1: Why is this Sprint valuable? — PO proposes how the product can increase value. The team crafts a Sprint Goal that makes the Sprint cohesive.

Topic 2: What can be done this Sprint? — Developers select items from the Product Backlog to include in the current Sprint, using their past performance (velocity) as a guide. They commit to a Sprint Goal, not to specific backlog items.

Topic 3: How will the chosen work get done? — For each selected Product Backlog item, the Developers plan how to create an Increment. Work is decomposed into items of one day or less. This creates the Sprint Backlog.

Output: Sprint Goal (commitment) + Sprint Backlog (plan) + understanding of how the team will self-organize.
☀️
Daily Scrum (Daily Standup)
Max 15 minutes · Every day · Developers only
A 15-minute event for the Developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the Sprint Backlog as necessary. The Daily Scrum is NOT a status report to the Scrum Master or management.

Traditional three questions (still useful but not required by the 2020 Guide):
— What did I do yesterday that helped the team meet the Sprint Goal?
— What will I do today to help the team meet the Sprint Goal?
— Do I see any impediment that prevents me or the team from meeting the Sprint Goal?

Key rules:
— Same time, same place, every day — creates predictability and reduces cognitive overhead
— Developers only (PO and SM attend only if they are doing development work)
— Not a problem-solving session — just surface problems; solve them after in smaller groups
— Standing improves focus and keeps it short (hence "standup")

Anti-pattern: Daily Scrum becomes a status report to the SM or manager ("telling boss what you did"). This kills psychological safety and defeats the purpose.
🎯
Sprint Review
Max 4 hours per month · End of sprint · Team + Stakeholders
The Scrum Team and stakeholders inspect the outcome of the Sprint and determine future adaptations. The team demonstrates working software to stakeholders and gathers feedback.

What happens:
— Scrum Team presents what was accomplished and what has changed in their environment
— Stakeholders discuss and provide feedback (this is the key value of the event)
— The Product Backlog is updated to reflect new insights
— Attendees collaborate on what to do next

What it is NOT: A demo performance or approval gate. Not a pass/fail review. It's a collaborative working session to determine what is the most valuable next step.

Output: A revised Product Backlog, updated Product Goal understanding, stakeholder feedback incorporated.
🔄
Sprint Retrospective
Max 3 hours per month · End of sprint · Scrum Team only
The team inspects how the last Sprint went with regards to individuals, interactions, processes, tools, and their Definition of Done. The goal is to identify improvements for the next Sprint.

Classic format (one of many):
— What went well? Practices, interactions, tools that worked — keep doing these
— What could be improved? Problems, inefficiencies, frustrations — be specific
— What will we commit to doing differently next Sprint? Concrete, specific, measurable action items

Key rules:
— Safe space: psychological safety is essential. No blame, no management present (unless they're part of the team)
— Actionable output: leave with 1–3 specific improvements to attempt next Sprint
— Follow-up: start the next retro by reviewing whether last Sprint's improvements worked

Other formats: Sailboat (wind/anchors), Mad/Sad/Glad, Start/Stop/Continue, 4Ls (Liked/Learned/Lacked/Longed For).

Most commonly skipped and most valuable event. Teams that do great retrospectives consistently outperform those that don't.
Scrum Deep Dive · Artifacts

Scrum Artifacts

Scrum defines three artifacts, each with a commitment. Artifacts represent work or value and are designed to maximize transparency of key information so that the team and stakeholders have the same understanding of the artifact.

Product Backlog
The ordered list of everything that is known to be needed in the product. Single source of truth for changes. Only product backlog items that meet the Definition of Ready enter a Sprint. Commitment: Product Goal.
Sprint Backlog
The Sprint Goal (why) + the set of Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint (what) + the actionable plan for delivering the Increment (how). Created by Developers. Commitment: Sprint Goal.
Increment
A concrete stepping stone toward the Product Goal. Each Increment is additive and must work with all previous Increments. Must meet the Definition of Done. Multiple Increments per Sprint is allowed. Commitment: Definition of Done.

Product Backlog — Deep Dive

The Product Backlog is an emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product. It is never complete — as long as a product exists, so does its Product Backlog.

PropertyDescription
Ordered, not prioritizedItems are ordered top-to-bottom. Higher-ordered items are clearer, smaller, and delivered sooner. Lower items are vaguer and larger.
Single source of truthOne backlog per product. Multiple teams share one Product Backlog (not per-team backlogs).
Owned by the POOnly the PO can order and remove items. Developers can suggest items but PO decides priority.
Living documentConstantly refined (groomed). Items are added, removed, reordered, and estimated continuously.
RefinementPBIs are broken down and clarified. Should be an ongoing activity consuming no more than 10% of sprint capacity.

Definition of Done (DoD)

The Definition of Done is a formal description of the state of the Increment when it meets the quality measures required for the product. It creates a shared understanding of what "done" means and prevents half-finished work from being counted.

If an item doesn't meet the DoD, it is NOT done — regardless of how much work was spent on it. It should not be presented at the Sprint Review and should not be counted toward velocity.
Example Definition of Done

Scrum Deep Dive · Backlog Items

User Stories & Estimation

User stories are the most common format for expressing Product Backlog Items (PBIs). Estimation techniques (story points, planning poker) determine how much work fits in a sprint and enable velocity tracking.

What is a User Story?

A User Story is an informal, natural-language description of a feature from the perspective of the user who desires it. It focuses on who wants it, what they want, and why — not how to build it.

Format: "As a [type of user], I want [some goal] so that [some reason/value]."
User Story Examples
// Good User Stories As a registered user, I want to reset my password via email, So that I can regain access to my account if I forget it. As a product manager, I want to filter the sales dashboard by date range, So that I can compare performance across time periods. As a mobile app user, I want to receive push notifications for order updates, So that I know the status of my delivery without opening the app. // Bad User Story (technical, no user perspective) As a system, I need to update the user table with a reset token when the /auth/reset-password endpoint is called.

INVEST Criteria — Good User Stories

I — Independent
Stories should not depend on each other. Avoids prioritization problems and partial completion issues.
N — Negotiable
Stories are not contracts. They are a starting point for conversation. Details are negotiated between PO and developers.
V — Valuable
Every story must deliver value to the user or business. No stories that are purely technical infrastructure (these are tasks or enablers).
E — Estimable
The team must be able to estimate its size. If they can't, it's too vague or too large (an Epic). Split it.
S — Small
A story should be completable within a Sprint (ideally in a few days). Large stories (Epics) must be split before entering a sprint.
T — Testable
Must have clear acceptance criteria so the PO and team can verify completion. "User can login" must have testable conditions.

Acceptance Criteria

Acceptance criteria define the conditions that must be met for a user story to be considered complete. They are written by the PO (with developer input) before the sprint and used to verify completion.

Acceptance Criteria Example
Story: As a user, I want to reset my password via email. Acceptance Criteria: Given I am on the login page When I click "Forgot Password" Then I see a form asking for my email address Given I enter a valid registered email When I submit the form Then I receive a reset email within 2 minutes And the email contains a unique, single-use link Given I click the reset link (valid, unexpired) When the link opens Then I can enter and confirm a new password Given I enter a password < 8 characters When I submit Then I see an error: "Password must be at least 8 characters" Given I use an expired link (24h) When I visit it Then I see: "This link has expired. Please request a new one."

Story Points & Estimation

Story points are a unit of measure for the relative complexity and effort of user stories — not hours. They capture size, complexity, risk, and uncertainty together. A story that's "twice as complex" gets twice the points.

Why not use hours? Hours are personal (what takes me 2 hours may take you 8). Hours create a false sense of precision. Story points measure relative complexity, which teams are much better at estimating than absolute time.

Planning Poker

Planning Poker is the most common estimation technique. Each team member votes simultaneously using a deck of cards (Fibonacci numbers). Outliers explain their reasoning, discussion follows, and the team re-votes until consensus is reached.

1
PO reads the user story
PO describes the story and answers clarifying questions. The team understands what is being built and why.
2
Team discusses briefly
Developers ask clarifying questions about scope, acceptance criteria, technical approach, and unknowns. Keep this to 2–3 minutes.
3
Simultaneous reveal
Everyone holds up their card simultaneously. Prevents anchoring bias — the first estimate affects everyone else's if not done simultaneously.
4
Outliers explain
The highest and lowest estimators explain their reasoning. This surfaces hidden assumptions and risks that others hadn't considered.
5
Re-vote until consensus
After discussion, the team votes again. Usually converges in 1–2 rounds. The goal is shared understanding, not false precision.

Kanban — Flow-Based Agile

Kanban is a method for managing work by visualizing it on a board, limiting the amount of work in progress at any given time, and managing flow. Unlike Scrum, it has no sprints, no fixed roles, and no prescribed ceremonies — making it highly adaptable.

Origin
Toyota 1940s
Software version
2007
Timebox
None
Key metric
Lead Time

The Six Core Practices of Kanban

1
Visualize the workflow
Make all work visible on the Kanban board. Columns represent workflow stages (Backlog → In Progress → Review → Done). Work items (cards) move left to right. Problems become visible when cards pile up or stop moving.
2
Limit Work In Progress (WIP)
The core insight of Kanban. Each column has a WIP limit — the maximum number of items allowed in that column simultaneously. Exceeding WIP limits means the team must finish existing work before starting new work. This prevents multitasking, reduces context-switching, and improves quality. If In Progress has WIP limit of 3 and 3 cards are there, you must finish one before starting another.
3
Manage flow
Track how work moves through the system. Identify bottlenecks (where cards pile up). The goal is smooth, predictable flow — not maximizing utilization of individuals. A developer who is "100% busy" but blocking QA is creating waste, not value.
4
Make policies explicit
Define clearly: What does it mean for an item to move from column to column? What are the entry and exit criteria for each stage? What is our Definition of Done? Explicit policies reduce ambiguity and enable self-organization.
5
Implement feedback loops
Regular cadence meetings: daily standup (optional), replenishment meeting (add items to backlog), delivery planning, service delivery review (review metrics), operations review. Kanban doesn't mandate these — it encourages them as feedback loops.
6
Improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally
Use models, metrics, and the scientific method to improve. Run small experiments. Measure the impact. Keep what works, discard what doesn't. Kanban's philosophy is evolutionary change — improve your current process rather than revolutionary transformation.

Kanban Metrics

Lead Time
The time from when a customer request is made (item enters the backlog) to when it is delivered. This is what the customer experiences. Reduce lead time to improve customer satisfaction.

Formula: Lead Time = Delivery Date − Request Date
Cycle Time
The time from when work actually starts on an item to when it's done. This is what the team controls. Shorter cycle time = faster throughput. Reduce cycle time by removing blockers and limiting WIP.

Formula: Cycle Time = Done Date − Work Start Date
Throughput
The number of items completed per unit of time (e.g., per week). Higher throughput means the team delivers more. Forecasting using throughput is often more accurate than story points.
Cumulative Flow Diagram (CFD)
A stacked area chart showing the count of items in each stage over time. Bands widening = items piling up in that stage (bottleneck). Parallel bands = smooth flow. Essential Kanban visualization.

Scrum vs Kanban — Quick Comparison

🏉 Scrum

  • Fixed-length sprints (1–4 weeks)
  • Three defined roles (PO, SM, Devs)
  • Sprint planning and retrospectives mandated
  • Story points and velocity tracking
  • Cannot change sprint scope once committed
  • Best for new product development
  • Teams with relatively stable composition
  • Strong product direction needed

📋 Kanban

  • Continuous flow — no iterations
  • No prescribed roles
  • No mandatory ceremonies
  • Lead time and throughput metrics
  • New items can enter at any time
  • Best for operations, support, maintenance
  • Teams where work type varies widely
  • Evolutionary change preferred

Extreme Programming (XP)

XP is the most engineering-focused Agile methodology. While Scrum focuses on the management process, XP focuses on software engineering practices that make the code easy to change — which is the prerequisite for any Agile approach to work.

Kent Beck's insight: "If code reviews are good, let's do them all the time (pair programming). If testing is good, let's test all the time (TDD). If integration is good, let's integrate all the time (continuous integration)." XP takes best practices to extremes.

XP's 5 Values

Communication
Constant, open communication between team members and with the customer. Daily contact. No information hiding.
Simplicity
Do the simplest thing that could possibly work. YAGNI — don't build what you don't need right now.
Feedback
Rapid feedback from tests, customers, and the team. Short iterations. TDD gives immediate feedback on every change.
Courage
Courage to refactor mercilessly. Courage to throw away code. Courage to say "this won't work" early. Courage to face hard truths.
Respect
Team members respect each other and the customer. No ego. Collective code ownership means anyone can improve any code.

XP's Key Engineering Practices

Test-Driven Development (TDD) +
Write the failing test first, then write the minimum code to make it pass, then refactor. The cycle: Red → Green → Refactor.

Benefits: Forces clarity about what the code should do before writing it. Creates a comprehensive test suite. Makes refactoring safe. Reduces debugging time significantly.

The TDD cycle:
1. Write a failing test (Red) — define what you want the code to do
2. Write the minimum code to pass (Green) — just enough, no more
3. Refactor (Refactor) — clean up without changing behavior
4. Repeat for the next requirement
Pair Programming +
Two developers work together at one keyboard. One "drives" (writes code), the other "navigates" (reviews, thinks about design, spots bugs). Roles switch frequently.

Benefits: Real-time code review (catches bugs immediately). Knowledge transfer (both learn). Collective ownership (the code belongs to neither — it belongs to the team). Improved design decisions through discussion.

Common objection: "We're paying two people to do one person's work." Studies show pair programming reduces bugs by 15% and increases team knowledge, resulting in faster overall throughput. The math often works out.
Continuous Integration (CI) +
Every developer integrates their code into the shared repository multiple times per day. Each integration is verified by automated tests. Problems are found and fixed immediately — not accumulated for weeks.

The rule: If the build is broken, fixing it is the team's highest priority. A broken build stops everyone's work from being verifiable.

Tools: GitHub Actions, Jenkins, CircleCI, GitLab CI. The CI server runs tests on every commit and alerts the team immediately if something breaks.
Refactoring +
Continuously improve the design of existing code without changing its behavior. Refactoring is not optional in XP — it's a discipline. The goal: the simplest possible design that passes all tests.

Why constantly? Code that never gets refactored accumulates technical debt — it becomes harder and harder to change. In Agile, code must always be easy to change (that's the whole point). Refactoring keeps the cost of change flat over time.

Safe to refactor when: You have a comprehensive test suite (hence why TDD comes first).
Small Releases & Collective Ownership +
Small Releases: Release to production frequently (every iteration or more). Each release should be the smallest possible that makes business sense. Reduces risk, provides feedback earlier, keeps morale high through regular wins.

Collective Code Ownership: Any developer can change any part of the codebase at any time. No "this is Bob's module." Everyone is responsible for everything. Combined with CI and TDD, this prevents knowledge silos and bus factor problems.

Coding Standards: The whole team agrees on a consistent style. Essential for collective ownership — you can't own code you can't read.

Lean Software Development

Lean is a philosophy derived from the Toyota Production System (TPS). In software, Lean means eliminating all forms of waste, amplifying learning, and delivering fast. Kanban is a Lean tool. Lean thinking underlies much of Agile.

The 7 Principles of Lean Software (Poppendieck)

#PrincipleWhat it means in software
1Eliminate WasteWaste = anything that doesn't add value. In software: unused features, waiting, unnecessary handoffs, task switching, defects, over-processing, extra inventory (partially done work).
2Amplify LearningSoftware is a learning process. Short iterations, frequent feedback, experiments, and retrospectives all amplify learning. Make knowledge visible and shareable.
3Decide as Late as PossibleDelay irreversible decisions until the last responsible moment — when you have the most information. Don't over-architect for requirements that may never exist.
4Deliver as Fast as PossibleFast delivery = faster feedback = less risk. Speed is a competitive advantage. The faster you deliver, the sooner you learn if you're building the right thing.
5Empower the TeamDecisions should be made by the people closest to the work. Managers set direction; teams choose how. Autonomy, mastery, and purpose drive performance.
6Build Integrity InQuality is not something you add at the end — it's built in from the start. TDD, refactoring, code reviews, and CI are integrity practices.
7See the WholeOptimize the entire value stream, not local sub-processes. A team that completes features 2x faster but creates bottlenecks in QA has not improved the system.

The 7 Wastes of Software (Lean)

Partially Done Work
Code written but not tested, not reviewed, not deployed. Inventory that doesn't flow. The most common and dangerous waste in software.
Extra Features
80% of features are rarely or never used (Pareto). Building them wastes time, adds complexity, and must be maintained forever.
Relearning
Solving the same problem repeatedly because solutions aren't captured. Poor documentation, no knowledge base, high turnover.
Handoffs
Every handoff between people/teams loses information. BA → Dev → QA → Ops is a chain of handoffs and information loss.
Task Switching
Developers working on 5 things simultaneously complete all 5 later than if they worked sequentially. Context switching is extremely costly.
Delays / Waiting
Waiting for approval, waiting for another team, waiting for information. Every wait is waste. WIP limits and single-piece flow reduce waiting.

FDD & DSDM

Feature-Driven Development (FDD) and Dynamic Systems Development Method (DSDM) are two less common but important Agile frameworks — FDD is feature-centric and model-heavy; DSDM is governance-heavy and popular in UK enterprise/government contexts.

Feature-Driven Development (FDD)

FDD is an iterative and incremental software development process. Developed by Jeff De Luca and Peter Coad. FDD is more prescriptive than other Agile methods — it defines specific roles, activities, and outputs.

1
Develop an Overall Model
Domain experts and developers create a high-level object model of the domain. Not a full design — a shared understanding map. Done once upfront.
2
Build a Features List
Decompose the model into a comprehensive feature list. Features are tiny, client-valued functions: "<action> the <result> <by/for/of/to> a <object>." E.g., "Calculate the total sales for a customer." Features take <2 weeks to build.
3
Plan By Feature
Sequence features based on business priority and technical dependencies. Assign feature sets to Chief Programmers (leads). Create a schedule for when feature sets will be built.
4
Design By Feature
For each feature: create sequence diagrams, refine object model, write class and method prologs. 1-week design activity before development.
5
Build By Feature
Implement the feature. Code, unit test, inspect. Feature builds take 1–10 days maximum. Regular client-visible feature builds every 2 weeks.

DSDM (Dynamic Systems Development Method)

DSDM is the oldest Agile framework (1994). It is the only Agile method that covers the entire project lifecycle including pre-project and post-project phases. It is strongly governance-focused and popular in the UK public sector.

DSDM's core principle: Fix time and cost; flex scope. Instead of saying "We'll build all these features; it might take 6–18 months," DSDM says "We'll deliver in 6 months for this budget; here's what we'll definitely build vs. what we might build."

Decision Guide · Key

When to Use What

Choosing the right Agile framework is not about what's most popular — it's about what fits your context. Team size, project type, organizational maturity, and requirements clarity all influence the right choice.

Decision Framework: Which Agile to Use

Q1
How many teams need to coordinate?
1 team: Scrum, Kanban, XP, or Crystal Clear depending on Q2–Q5.
2–9 teams: LeSS or Nexus (prefer product companies) / Scrumban if more flexible.
10–50+ teams: SAFe (enterprise), LeSS Huge (product companies), Scrum@Scale.
100+ teams: SAFe or Scrum@Scale are the only viable options.
Q2
What type of work is it?
New product / feature development: Scrum (best fit — structured, sprint-based).
Operations / support / maintenance: Kanban (continuous flow, no fixed sprints needed).
High-quality engineering with strong tech culture: XP or Scrum+XP hybrid.
Research / exploratory work: Kanban or Crystal Clear.
Q3
How stable are the requirements?
Requirements well-known and stable: Waterfall or DSDM may be appropriate.
Requirements clear for next month, fuzzy beyond: Scrum ideal — short sprints.
Requirements change day-to-day: Kanban — no need to lock into a sprint plan.
Requirements completely unknown: Lean Startup experiments + Kanban.
Q4
How mature is the team in Agile?
Brand new to Agile: Start with Scrum — most support, most training, most community.
Agile-experienced, wants more flexibility: Kanban or Scrumban.
Expert team, strong engineering culture: XP or minimal Scrum.
Organization needs change management support: SAFe (has built-in transformation playbook).
Q5
What are the organizational constraints?
Regulatory / compliance-heavy (government, finance, healthcare): SAFe or DSDM (built-in compliance documentation).
Fixed budget and time: DSDM (MoSCoW — flex scope, fix time/cost).
Startup / fast growth: Scrum or Kanban (lightweight, fast).
Legacy enterprise culture: SAFe provides the "enterprise-friendly" Agile transformation path.

Advanced · Combinations

Hybrid Approaches

Real-world teams rarely use a single framework in its pure form. Hybrids combine the best elements of multiple frameworks to fit their specific context. Understanding common hybrids helps you make pragmatic choices.

Scrumban

Scrumban is the most popular hybrid — combining Scrum's structure with Kanban's flow. Teams typically keep Scrum's events (standups, retrospectives, reviews) but add WIP limits and move away from strict sprint commitments.

Scrumban — What you take from each
From Scrum: Sprint cadence, daily standup, retrospectives, sprint reviews, velocity tracking (optional), user stories.

From Kanban: WIP limits, visual board with flow metrics, pull-based planning (pull items into sprint as capacity opens), no "sprint commitment" pressure, throughput measurement.

Best for: Teams transitioning away from pure Scrum, teams with high variability in work types, teams supporting products (mix of features + bugs + tech debt).

Scrum + XP

The most powerful combination for engineering teams. Scrum provides the management framework (sprints, PO, ceremonies); XP provides the engineering practices (TDD, CI, pair programming, refactoring) that make Agile sustainable.

Why this combination works: Scrum is intentionally silent about engineering practices — it only defines the process. XP fills the gap. Without engineering discipline, Scrum teams accumulate technical debt that eventually prevents them from delivering value quickly — defeating the entire purpose.

Agile + Waterfall (Phase-Gate Hybrid)

Some organizations use Waterfall for initial requirements and design phases, then switch to Agile for development. Or use Agile during development but require Waterfall-style formal approvals at key gates.

Warning: This often results in "Wagile" — the worst of both worlds. You get Waterfall's upfront planning rigidity and you don't get early delivery feedback that makes Agile valuable. This hybrid is usually a sign of organizational resistance to full Agile adoption, not a genuine best-of-both approach.

SAFe + Kanban at Team Level

Many organizations run SAFe at the program/portfolio level but allow individual teams to choose Scrum or Kanban at the team level. This is explicitly supported by SAFe — "Team Kanban" is a recognized SAFe pattern for teams whose work doesn't fit the Sprint-based model (e.g., operations, DevOps, support teams).

Implementation Guide

Implementing Agile

How do you actually transform a team or organization from traditional processes to Agile? This is where most implementations fail — not because of wrong framework choice, but because of poor change management and incomplete adoption.

Starting Scrum from Scratch — Step by Step

1
Define the Scrum Team
Identify and commit 3–9 developers. Assign a Product Owner (someone who understands the product vision and can prioritize). Assign a Scrum Master (ideally someone trained; don't just pick a developer and call them SM). The team should be dedicated — part-time Scrum almost always fails.
2
Create the Product Backlog
Product Owner creates an initial backlog of user stories/features. Doesn't need to be complete — start with the top 20–30 most important items detailed enough for Sprint Planning. Items should be written from the user's perspective, have acceptance criteria, and be estimable. Use Jira, Linear, Trello, or a whiteboard.
3
Agree on Definition of Done and working agreements
Team collectively defines: What does "done" mean? (code reviewed, tested, deployed to staging, accepted by PO). What are our norms? (hours, meetings, communication channels, coding standards). Write these down and put them somewhere visible. Revisit in retrospectives.
4
Run Sprint 1 Planning
PO presents top backlog items. Team asks clarifying questions. Team estimates complexity (story points). Team selects items they're confident they can complete. Team creates the Sprint Goal — one sentence that captures the sprint's purpose. First sprint: keep scope conservative — it's learning time.
5
Execute the Sprint
Daily standup at a fixed time. Update the board. SM removes impediments. PO is available to answer questions — not to add new work. Developers self-organize to accomplish the Sprint Goal. If you're going to miss the goal, raise it immediately — don't hide it until the review.
6
Sprint Review + Retrospective
Demo working software to stakeholders. Get feedback. Reprioritize the backlog. Then: hold the retrospective — even if you think it went perfectly. Especially for sprint 1, there's always something to improve. Take 1–2 concrete actions. Don't skip this.
7
Repeat and improve
Each sprint, you get better. Velocity stabilizes after 3–5 sprints. Process improvements from retrospectives compound. By sprint 10, the team you have is dramatically better than the team you started with. Track this — it's motivating.

Agile Transformation — Organizational Level

Scaling Agile beyond one team requires organizational change management. Most Agile transformations fail not because of the framework — but because of leadership, culture, and structure.

⚠️ Why Transformations Fail
Management doesn't change behavior (says "Agile" but still micromanages). Teams adopt ceremonies without the mindset. Annual budgeting processes contradict Lean budgeting. Functional silos (Dev, QA, Ops) prevent cross-functional teams. No executive sponsorship. Agile coaches leave and nothing sticks.
✅ Transformation Success Factors
Start with willing teams — don't force it. Get leadership to change first. Invest in training, coaching, and Communities of Practice. Change budgeting and HR processes to match Agile. Measure business outcomes, not process compliance. Be patient — 18–36 months for cultural change.

The Agile Adoption Maturity Model

Level 0: Pre-Agile
Waterfall or chaotic development. No iterations. Big design upfront. Testing at the end. Single large delivery.
Level 1: Agile theater ("ScrumBut")
"We do Scrum, BUT we don't do retrospectives." "We do Scrum, BUT sprints can be extended." Ceremonies adopted; mindset not changed. Teams go through motions.
Level 2: Agile by the book
Full Scrum ceremonies. Story points. Velocity tracked. PO engaged. SM facilitating. DoD enforced. Delivering every sprint. This is where most teams aim to be.
Level 3: High-performing Agile
Continuous delivery. TDD and CI/CD. Psychological safety high. Retrospective improvements actually compound. Team predicts velocity accurately. Technical excellence maintained.
Level 4: Business Agility
The whole organization is Agile — not just IT. Strategy adapts quarterly. Lean budgeting. Customer value drives everything. Product thinking replaces project thinking. This is the goal.

Agile + DevOps & CI/CD

The connection: Agile says "deliver working software frequently." CI/CD is the technical foundation that makes frequent delivery safe. Without automated testing and deployment pipelines, "frequent delivery" means frequent risk.

How Agile and DevOps reinforce each other

Sprint → Deploy cycle alignment
Every sprint ends with a potentially shippable increment. CI/CD makes "potentially shippable" mean "actually shippable." Teams move from "we could release this" to "we already did." Release frequency aligns with sprint cadence or faster.
Definition of Done includes CI/CD gates

Common Agile Pitfalls

Most Agile failures are not framework failures — they are adoption failures. Here are the most common anti-patterns, how to recognize them, and how to fix them.

🚩 "ScrumBut" — Doing Scrum, but not really +
What it looks like: "We do Scrum, but we skip retrospectives because we don't have time." "We do Scrum, but sprint scope changes every day." "We do Scrum, but the PO is not available."

Why it's a problem: You get the overhead of Scrum (meetings, ceremonies, roles) without the benefits (predictability, feedback loops, continuous improvement). ScrumBut teams often conclude "Scrum doesn't work" when really they never tried it.

Fix: Identify which Scrum elements you're violating. Ask: are you avoiding them because they're hard, or because they genuinely don't apply? Usually it's because they're hard — which is exactly when you need them most.
🚩 Proxy Product Owner — PO is not empowered +
What it looks like: The PO must "check with management" before making backlog decisions. The PO changes priorities daily based on whoever last spoke to them. Developers receive conflicting direction from multiple stakeholders.

Why it's a problem: The entire point of having a single PO is to have one voice, one decision-maker, one person who can say "yes" and "no." Without authority, the PO becomes a bottleneck and a gossip relay — not a value-maximizer.

Fix: The PO must have authority to prioritize the backlog without approval. Management provides direction and context; PO makes decisions. Senior leadership must explicitly delegate this authority.
🚩 Sprint = mini-Waterfall (no collaboration within sprint) +
What it looks like: Sprint starts with requirements. Days 1–5: developers write code. Days 6–9: QA tests. Day 10: PO reviews. This is Waterfall with a 2-week cycle, not Scrum.

Why it's a problem: All the problems of Waterfall (late defect discovery, hand-off waste, lack of collaboration) but compressed into 2 weeks. No benefit of cross-functional collaboration.

Fix: Each story should go through the full dev+test+review cycle within the sprint, concurrently where possible. QA and dev collaborate from the start of each story, not in sequence. PO reviews features as they're completed, not at the end.
🚩 Daily Scrum as status report to management +
What it looks like: The Scrum Master (or manager) asks each developer "What did you do yesterday?" Developers report to the SM rather than to each other. Team members feel anxiety about what they'll say.

Why it's a problem: Creates command-and-control culture. Developers optimize for looking productive instead of actually being productive. Information flow goes to the SM, not between developers. Trust is destroyed.

Fix: The Daily Scrum belongs to the Developers. The SM facilitates, doesn't interrogate. Focus on: "What helps us reach the Sprint Goal?" not "What did each person do?" Have developers speak to the board, not to the SM.
🚩 Velocity used to pressure teams (velocity gaming) +
What it looks like: Management tracks team velocity and asks teams to increase it. Teams inflate story points to show higher velocity. Teams rush work to hit velocity targets.

Why it's a problem: Velocity is a planning tool, not a performance metric. Comparing teams by velocity is meaningless (story points are relative to each team). Inflated estimates make sprint planning inaccurate. Technical debt accumulates from rushing.

Fix: Never compare velocities between teams. Never use velocity as a goal. Use it only for sprint capacity planning. Educate stakeholders: "higher velocity" doesn't mean "working harder" — it means "better at splitting stories into smaller pieces."
🚩 No technical practices → accumulating technical debt +
What it looks like: Team ships features fast for 6 months. Then progress slows dramatically. Every new feature takes 3x longer because of the mess underneath. Bugs multiply. Developers become afraid to change anything.

Why it's a problem: Agile requires the ability to change quickly. Technical debt removes that ability. A "fast" team that doesn't manage tech debt becomes a slow team within a year.

Fix: Add TDD to your Definition of Done. Allocate 20% of each sprint to tech debt / refactoring. Track technical debt as real backlog items. Add engineering practices from XP. The Scrum Master should surface this to management as a business risk.
🚩 Agile only in development — not in the organization +
What it looks like: Development team runs Scrum. But: annual budgeting cycles. Quarterly roadmaps committed to 12 months ahead. HR processes reward individual performance over team performance. Management makes architecture decisions.

Why it's a problem: The development team is trying to be Agile inside a Waterfall organization. The organizational processes override and undermine the team-level practices. This creates frustration and limits what Agile can deliver.

Fix: Agile transformations must change organizational processes, not just team processes. Lean budgeting (fund teams, not projects). OKRs instead of detailed annual plans. Change hiring and performance management to support Agile values. This is the hard part — and why Agile transformations take years.

Agile Vocabulary Quick Reference

Epic
A large user story that must be split before entering a sprint. Too big for one sprint. Parent of multiple smaller stories.
Feature
A user-facing capability, typically spanning multiple user stories. Parent of stories. Child of Epic.
User Story
"As a [user], I want [goal] so that [reason]." Fits in a sprint. Has acceptance criteria.
Task
Technical sub-unit of a user story. Typically hours, not points. Created by developers during Sprint Planning.
Spike
Time-boxed research/exploration story. Used to resolve technical uncertainty. Produces knowledge, not shippable code.
Bus Factor
The number of team members who, if hit by a bus, would halt the project. Collective ownership and pair programming raise the bus factor.
DoD
Definition of Done. Checklist a story must pass to be "done." Includes code review, tests, deployment.
DoR
Definition of Ready. Criteria a story must meet before it enters a sprint. Has acceptance criteria, estimated, no open questions.
Impediment
A blocker that prevents the team from making progress. The SM's job is to remove impediments the team cannot remove itself.
Technical Debt
Shortcuts and poor practices that make future changes harder. Like financial debt: accumulates interest over time.
YAGNI
You Ain't Gonna Need It. Don't build features you don't have an immediate need for. XP/Lean principle.
MVP
Minimum Viable Product. The smallest version of a product that delivers value to users and enables validated learning.
