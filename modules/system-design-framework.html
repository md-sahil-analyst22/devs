🏗️ System Design Interview
The Interview Framework
System design interviews test your ability to architect large-scale distributed systems. Most candidates fail not from lack of knowledge, but from lack of structure. This 5-step framework gives you a repeatable, confident approach every single time.
SYSTEM DESIGN INTERVIEW FRAMEWORK
A battle-tested 5-step framework used by engineers at Google, Meta, Amazon, and Netflix. Follow every step in order — each one builds on the previous. Skipping any step signals junior-level thinking to your interviewer.

The 5-Step Flow

Why Structure Beats Raw Knowledge

Shows Senior Thinking
Interviewers at FAANG evaluate HOW you think, not just what you know. A clear structured approach signals you've designed real production systems before.
Prevents Scope Creep
Most candidates start drawing boxes and rabbit-hole into one component. The framework forces scope clarity first — you'll never waste 30 minutes on the wrong problem.
Drives the Conversation
Following these steps puts YOU in control. You guide the interviewer through your reasoning at each stage — you're the architect, not a student being quizzed.
The #1 mistake in system design interviews: Starting to design without clarifying requirements. Interviewers intentionally give vague prompts like "Design Twitter." Always spend 5 minutes asking clarifying questions — it's the difference between passing and failing a senior-level interview.
❓ Step 1 of 5 — ~5 minutes
Deciding Requirements
Requirements define the boundaries and goals of your system. Every architecture decision you make later should trace back to a requirement. Get this wrong and you'll spend 40 minutes designing the wrong system.
Time Budget
5 minutes
Output
Written scope + constraints

Two Types of Requirements

📋 Functional Requirements
What the system does. User-facing behaviours and features. Think in terms of: what actions can users perform? What data flows in and out?

These are the features — "Users can post tweets", "Users can follow others", "Users see a feed". Write these as numbered bullet points on the board.
⚡ Non-Functional Requirements
How well the system does it. Quality attributes that constrain the architecture — availability, latency, consistency, durability, scalability.

These are the constraints — "99.99% uptime", "Timeline loads in <200ms", "10M DAU", "Messages never lost". These drive your technology choices.

The Questions to Ask (Script)

Scope: "What features are in scope?"
Don't try to design all of Twitter. Ask: "Should I design the full product or a specific subset like the timeline and posting?" Interviewers often want a focused design. Narrow scope = deeper design = better interview.
Scale: "How many users? What's the read vs write ratio?"
1K users vs 100M users are entirely different systems. Read-heavy (social feed: 100:1) vs write-heavy (logging: 1:100) changes every architectural decision. Always ask — never assume scale.
Consistency: "Is strong consistency required?"
Social timeline: eventual consistency OK (1-2s delay is fine). Bank balance: strong consistency required (never show wrong amount). This drives your CAP theorem choice and database selection.
SLA: "What are the availability and latency targets?"
99.9% uptime = 8.7 hours downtime/year. 99.99% = 52 minutes/year. 99.999% = 5 minutes/year. Each 9 costs dramatically more. Latency targets drive caching and CDN decisions.
Geography: "Single region or global?"
Global distribution changes everything — multi-region databases, cross-region replication, CDN edge nodes, data residency requirements. Single region is much simpler to start with.

Opening Script — Use This Word for Word

🎙️ Interview Script: Deciding Requirements
Open with:
"Before I start designing, let me clarify requirements so I'm solving the right problem."
Functional:
"What are the core features? Which should I prioritise? What's explicitly out of scope?"
Scale:
"How many DAU? What's the expected read/write ratio? Any seasonal spikes?"
Consistency:
"Is eventual consistency acceptable, or do we need strong consistency?"
Availability:
"What's the uptime SLA? What's the acceptable latency at p99?"
Close with:
"So to summarise: [repeat requirements]. I'll prioritise X, Y, Z and leave A, B out of scope. Does that sound right?"

Do

  • Spend a full 5 minutes — don't rush this step
  • Write all requirements visibly on the whiteboard

  • Ask about read vs write ratio — drives architecture
  • Confirm understanding before proceeding
  • Define the scale explicitly: "300M DAU, 100:1 ratio"

Don't

  • Start drawing boxes before requirements are clear
  • Assume scale — always ask even if it seems obvious
  • Try to design every feature — pick a focused subset
  • Skip non-functional requirements (often more important)
  • Forget to verify your understanding with the interviewer

👥 Step 2 · Metric 1 of 4
DAU & MAU — User Scale
DAU (Daily Active Users) and MAU (Monthly Active Users) are the foundational metrics in capacity estimation. They are your single starting number — every other calculation (RPS, storage, bandwidth) is derived directly from them.
👥 DAU / MAU
DAU = number of unique users who interact with your product on a given day.
MAU = number of unique users who interact with your product in a given month.
DAU/MAU ratio = product stickiness. 50%+ is excellent; below 20% means users aren't habitual.

What DAU and MAU Tell You

Scale of Infrastructure
1K DAU = a single server. 1M DAU = a small cluster. 100M DAU = hundreds of servers, sharding, CDN, global edge nodes. DAU is the primary scaling driver for every component.
Product Engagement
DAU/MAU = stickiness ratio. WhatsApp: ~90% (people use it daily). E-commerce: ~5% (people shop monthly). This ratio informs read/write patterns and peak load assumptions.
Business Validation
The interviewer gives you DAU to validate your capacity estimate. "Does this system need 1000 servers or 10?" The answer starts with: "We have 100M DAU, so..."

DAU → RPS Conversion Formula

The Core Formula
RPS = (DAU × actions_per_user_per_day) ÷ 86,400

Read_RPS = (DAU × reads_per_user) ÷ 86,400 Write_RPS = (DAU × writes_per_user) ÷ 86,400

Peak_RPS = Average_RPS × 2 to 3 (traffic spikes in mornings/evenings) Example: 300M DAU, 100 reads/day, 1 write/day → Read RPS = 300M × 100 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 347,000 RPS Write RPS = 300M × 1 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 3,470 RPS Peak read = 347,000 × 3 ≈ 1,000,000 RPS

⚡ Step 2 · Metric 2 of 4
Throughput — RPS & QPS
Throughput is the number of requests your system must handle per second. It's the metric that directly determines how many servers you need, whether you need a cache, and how to design your load balancer. Always separate reads from writes.
⚡ Throughput / RPS
RPS = Requests Per Second — total inbound requests your system handles.
QPS = Queries Per Second — database-specific term, same concept.
TPS = Transactions Per Second — used for write-heavy or financial systems.
Always calculate Read RPS and Write RPS separately — they drive different infrastructure.

Throughput Formulas

Throughput Calculations
Read_RPS = (DAU × avg_reads_per_user) ÷ 86,400 Write_RPS = (DAU × avg_writes_per_user) ÷ 86,400 Apply peak multiplier: Peak_RPS = Average_RPS × 2–3 (morning/evening spikes) Derive server count: Servers_needed = Peak_RPS ÷ RPS_per_server 1 app server ≈ 1K–5K RPS (depends on CPU/memory workload) DB servers (after cache offload): DB_RPS = Read_RPS × (1 - cache_hit_rate) With 95% cache hit: DB sees only 5% of read traffic

Worked Example — Twitter Throughput

── TWITTER THROUGHPUT ESTIMATION ─────────────────────── DAU = 300M users Avg reads/user/day = 100 (scroll timeline, view tweets) Avg writes/user/day = 1 (post 1 tweet per day on average) Read RPS = 300M × 100 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 347,000 RPS Write RPS = 300M × 1 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 3,470 RPS Read:Write ratio ≈ 100:1 Peak (3× multiplier): Peak Read RPS ≈ ~1,000,000 RPS Peak Write RPS ≈ ~10,000 RPS ── SERVER SIZING ──────────────────────────────────────── API servers (1K RPS each) ≈ 1,000 servers (naive, no cache) With 95% cache hit rate: DB-hitting Read RPS ≈ 17,350 RPS DB read replicas (10K QPS each) ≈ 2–3 read replicas DB primary (write only) ≈ 1 primary (10K WPS capacity)
Cache impact on throughput: A 95% cache hit rate reduces DB load from 347K RPS to just 17K RPS — a 20× reduction. This is why caching is the single most impactful optimization. Always mention cache hit rate when discussing throughput in interviews.
💾 Step 2 · Metric 3 of 4
Storage & Memory Estimation
Storage estimation tells you how much disk space and memory your system needs now and in the future. It's the key driver for database sharding decisions, object storage vs relational DB choices, and whether you need tiered storage (hot/warm/cold).
💾 Storage / Memory
Storage = persistent disk space for your data (database rows, media files, logs).
Memory (RAM) = fast in-memory space for caches, sessions, queues (Redis, Memcached).
Always estimate metadata separately from media — media is typically 1000× larger but stored differently (S3 vs DB).

Storage Formulas

Storage Calculation Chain
Step 1 — Daily storage growth: Storage_per_day = Write_RPS × 86,400 × avg_object_size Step 2 — Long-term storage: Total_storage = Storage_per_day × 365 × years_retention Step 3 — Account for replication: Physical_storage = Total_storage × replication_factor (typically 3) Step 4 — Cache memory sizing: Cache_RAM = hot_data_fraction × total_working_set_size The "80/20 rule": 20% of data gets 80% of reads → cache that 20%

Storage Tiers — Hot / Warm / Cold

🔥 Hot Storage — In-Memory (Redis, RAM)
Accessed in milliseconds. Extremely fast but expensive (~$10/GB vs $0.02/GB for cold storage). Store: session tokens, timeline caches, trending data, rate-limiting counters. Typically keep last 30 days of hot data.
☀️ Warm Storage — SSD Database (Postgres, Cassandra)
Accessed in milliseconds to tens of milliseconds. Balance of speed and cost. Store: active user records, recent posts, metadata, business data. Index carefully for your query patterns.
❄️ Cold Storage — Object Store (S3, GCS Nearline)
Accessed in seconds. Very cheap ($0.004/GB/month). Store: media files (images, videos), archived posts, audit logs, backups, ML training data. Never query this with millisecond SLA expectations.

Worked Example — Instagram Storage

── INSTAGRAM STORAGE ESTIMATION ───────────────────────── DAU = 500M · Photo uploads/day = 100M photos (20% of users) Avg photo size (compressed) = 3MB Thumbnails (5 sizes per photo) = 3MB × 0.3 = ~1MB extra ── DAILY GROWTH ───────────────────────────────────────── Photo storage/day = 100M × 3MB = 300 TB/day Thumbnail storage/day= 100M × 1MB = 100 TB/day Metadata (DB rows) = 100M × 5KB = 500 GB/day Total raw = 300 + 100 TB ≈ 400 TB/day ── LONG-TERM (5 years, 3× replication) ────────────────── 5-year storage = 400TB × 365 × 5 ≈ 730 PB raw With 3× replication ≈ ~2.2 Exabytes Stored on S3 (cold) @ $0.023/GB/month ≈ ~$50M/month ── CACHE MEMORY SIZING ────────────────────────────────── Hot data (last 7 days feed metadata only) 100M posts × 7 days × 5KB ≈ 3.5 TB RAM Redis cluster (~100GB/node) ≈ ~35 Redis nodes
Key insight: Split metadata (fast DB, small) from media (slow object store, enormous). Instagram's DB stores 5KB of metadata per photo. The 3MB photo goes to S3. This separation is fundamental — mention it explicitly in interviews.
🌐 Step 2 · Metric 4 of 4
Network & Bandwidth
Bandwidth is the volume of data transferred per second. It drives CDN decisions, justifies edge caching, determines internal network hardware requirements, and reveals whether your system needs content offloading. Outbound bandwidth almost always dominates.
🌐 Network / Bandwidth
Inbound bandwidth = data flowing INTO your system (uploads, write requests). Driven by Write RPS.
Outbound bandwidth = data flowing OUT of your system (downloads, read responses). Driven by Read RPS — usually 10–100× inbound.
Outbound is expensive (cloud egress fees) and is the primary justification for CDNs.

Bandwidth Formulas

Bandwidth Calculation
Inbound_BW = Write_RPS × avg_request_payload_size Outbound_BW = Read_RPS × avg_response_payload_size Peak bandwidth: Peak_BW = Average_BW × 2–3 (same peak multiplier as RPS) After CDN offload (assumes 90%+ of static traffic served from edge): Origin_BW = Outbound_BW × (1 - cdn_offload_ratio) CDN offloads ~90–99% of static content. Origin handles dynamic only.

Inbound vs Outbound — Why Outbound Dominates

📤 Inbound (Upload)
Users write text (300 bytes), occasionally upload photos (3 MB) or videos (50+ MB). Inbound bandwidth is usually small — very few users upload compared to how many users read.

Twitter example: 3,470 write RPS × 300 bytes = ~1 MB/s inbound (trivial)
📥 Outbound (Download)
Every user viewing their feed downloads text + thumbnails. Video streaming multiplies this enormously. Outbound is always the bottleneck and the CDN justification.

Twitter example: 347K read RPS × 300 bytes = ~100 MB/s just for text feeds

Worked Example — YouTube Bandwidth

── YOUTUBE BANDWIDTH ESTIMATION ───────────────────────── DAU = 122M · Avg watch time = 40 min/user/day Video bitrate (1080p) = 5 Mbps ── INBOUND (Uploads) ──────────────────────────────────── Uploads/day = 500 hours of video per minute = 720,000 hrs/day Avg 1hr video = 2GB after processing Raw inbound BW = 720,000 × 2GB ÷ 86,400 ≈ ~17 GB/s inbound ── OUTBOUND (Streams) ─────────────────────────────────── Concurrent viewers = 122M × (40min ÷ 1440min) ≈ 3.4M concurrent Outbound BW = 3.4M × 5Mbps ≈ ~17 Tbps (raw) ── CDN OFFLOAD ────────────────────────────────────────── CDN offloads ~99% of video delivery Origin bandwidth ≈ ~170 Gbps CDN serves ≈ ~16.8 Tbps ── COST IMPLICATION ───────────────────────────────────── Without CDN @ $0.09/GB: 17 Tbps × $0.09 ≈ $~200M/day With CDN @ $0.02/GB ≈ $~45M/day CDN saves YouTube ≈ $55B+/year ← cost justification

Network Topology — Where Bandwidth Matters

Client ↔ CDN Edge Node (~50ms, 1–10 Gbps per edge)
Serve all static content (images, videos, JS, CSS) from CDN. Users get the nearest edge node, not your origin. This is your first and most impactful bandwidth optimisation.
CDN Edge ↔ Origin (~50–150ms, 1–100 Gbps)
CDN pulls content from your origin on first request, then serves from cache. Origin handles only cache misses (typically <1% of requests for popular content).
App Server ↔ Database (<1ms same DC, 10 Gbps internal)
Internal bandwidth is cheap and fast. Use 10 Gbps NIC on DB servers. Minimize large result sets with pagination — don't SELECT * when you need 5 columns.
Cross-Region Replication (50–150ms, expensive egress)
AWS charges $0.02–0.09/GB for cross-region data transfer. Multi-region active/passive adds ~5–15% to DB storage costs. Design replication to be async and batched where consistency allows.
Interview answer pattern: When asked about a video platform, say: "The outbound bandwidth is enormous — 3.4M concurrent viewers at 5Mbps each is ~17 Tbps. We absolutely need a CDN to offload 99% of this. The CDN also moves content closer to users, reducing latency from 200ms to <20ms. Without it, origin bandwidth costs alone would be unsustainable."
🔌 Step 3 of 5 — ~10 minutes
API Design
API design translates functional requirements into concrete endpoints. Defining the API before drawing architecture forces you to think in terms of data contracts — what flows in, what flows out — which directly reveals which components you need and how they communicate.

Why API Design Comes Before Architecture

The API is the Contract
Think of the API as a signed contract between your system and its clients. Once published, you can't break it without versioning. Defining it first forces crisp thinking about: what data does each operation need? How granular should operations be? What's the payload shape? These questions reveal your data model and component boundaries before you've drawn a single box.

REST API Principles

Nouns as Resources, HTTP Methods as Verbs
GET /tweets/{id} — not GET /getTweet. URL = the resource (noun). HTTP method = the action (verb). GET = read. POST = create. PUT/PATCH = update. DELETE = delete.
Cursor-Based Pagination on All List Endpoints
?limit=20&cursor=lastSeenId. Never return unbounded lists (DB will die). Cursor-based > offset-based for real-time feeds — no duplicate/missed items when new content arrives during pagination.
Version from Day One: /v1/
Always prefix with /v1/. When you need breaking changes, ship /v2/ while keeping /v1/ alive. Clients on old versions keep working. Never silently break external clients.
Idempotency Keys on All Writes
Header: Idempotency-Key: uuid-v4. Client sends the same key on retry after network failure. Server deduplicates using the key — returns the same result without creating a duplicate. Critical for payment APIs.

Complete Twitter API Example

─── TWEETS ────────────────────────────────────────────── POST /v1/tweets Headers: Authorization: Bearer <jwt> Idempotency-Key: <uuid> Body: { text: string(280), mediaIds?: string[], replyToId?: string } Returns: 201 { tweetId, text, author, createdAt } GET /v1/tweets/{tweetId} Returns: 200 { id, text, author, likeCount, retweetCount, createdAt } DELETE /v1/tweets/{tweetId} // 403 if not owner Returns: 204 No Content ─── TIMELINE ──────────────────────────────────────────── GET /v1/timeline/home Query: ?limit=20&cursor={lastSeenTweetId} Returns: 200 { tweets: Tweet[], nextCursor: string, hasMore: bool } GET /v1/timeline/user/{userId} Query: ?limit=20&cursor={cursor} ─── SOCIAL GRAPH ──────────────────────────────────────── POST /v1/users/{userId}/follow // 200 or 409 already following DELETE /v1/users/{userId}/follow // 200 or 404 GET /v1/users/{userId}/followers // paginated list ─── INTERACTIONS ──────────────────────────────────────── POST /v1/tweets/{tweetId}/like DELETE /v1/tweets/{tweetId}/like POST /v1/tweets/{tweetId}/retweet
Interview tip: You don't need to list every endpoint. Show 5–7 core endpoints that demonstrate you understand the data model and access patterns. Then say: "I'll add authentication headers, rate limiting, and pagination throughout. These endpoints cover the core functional requirements we defined."
🏛️ Step 4 of 5 — ~10 minutes
High-Level Design
The high-level design is your system's architecture at the component level. You're drawing how data flows through the main building blocks — not implementation details. Narrate while you draw: "Requests enter via the load balancer, hit the API Gateway for auth, then route to stateless app servers..."

The Standard Architecture

Components Explained

🌐 CDN
Serves static assets (JS/CSS/images/video) from 200+ edge nodes worldwide. Reduces origin bandwidth by 90–99%. Users get <20ms latency instead of 150ms from origin.
⚖️ Load Balancer
Distributes traffic across app servers. Auto-removes unhealthy servers. Enables seamless horizontal scaling — add servers without touching DNS. Use Layer 7 (ALB) for microservices routing.
🚪 API Gateway
Validates JWT tokens once for all services. Enforces rate limits (1000 req/min per API key). Terminates SSL. Routes /users/* to User Service, /orders/* to Order Service.
🖥️ App Servers
Stateless — any server handles any request. Store no session state in memory. Auto-scale group adds/removes instances based on CPU. Target: 60–70% CPU utilization.
📬 Message Queue
Decouples writes from background processing. App writes to queue and responds instantly. Workers process async (email, notifications, encoding). Absorbs traffic spikes without dropping requests.
⚡ Cache (Redis)
<1ms reads. Cache hot data: user profiles, timelines, sessions. Cache-aside pattern. 95% hit rate offloads 95% of DB read traffic. TTL + explicit invalidation on writes.
While drawing: Narrate each component and WHY it's there. Don't silently draw boxes. Say: "I'm adding a Redis cache here because our read:write ratio is 100:1 and the DB can't handle 350K read RPS. With a 95% cache hit rate, only 17K RPS reach the database — well within Postgres capacity."

Deep Dive
The deep dive is where you earn the senior-level evaluation. You identify bottlenecks, justify every design decision with numbers, and articulate tradeoffs explicitly. This is where candidates get differentiated — shallow responses lose, deep justified decisions win.

The 6 Deep Dive Focus Areas

1. Database Schema & Indexing
Show key tables, primary keys, indexes. Explain: "Timeline queries filter by user_id and sort by created_at DESC — composite index (user_id, created_at DESC) reduces scan from O(n) to O(log n)." Connect index choices to your access patterns.
2. Feed Generation — Push vs Pull vs Hybrid
Push (fan-out on write): pre-populate follower timelines when a tweet is posted. O(1) reads, O(followers) writes. Breaks for celebrities with 50M followers.
Pull (fan-out on read): merge feeds at query time. O(following) reads, O(1) writes. Too slow at scale.
Hybrid: push for <1M followers, pull for celebrities. Twitter uses this.
3. Caching Strategy
What to cache (hot reads: timelines, profiles, sessions), what TTL (session: 24h, timeline: 60s, product: 5min), how to invalidate (DEL key on write, event-driven invalidation via Kafka), how to handle stampede (mutex lock + TTL jitter ±5%).
4. Database Sharding & Replication
Add read replicas first (reads = 90% of DB traffic). Shard by user_id (hash-based, consistent hashing) only when write throughput exceeds primary DB capacity. Cross-shard joins = impossible, so denormalise early.
5. Failure Scenarios & Reliability
Walk through: DB down → replica promoted (60s downtime), writes queued in MQ. Cache fails → circuit breaker falls back to DB, potential stampede mitigated by jitter. MQ full → back-pressure via 429 to API, auto-scale consumers.
6. CAP Theorem Trade-off
State explicitly: "For the timeline, eventual consistency is fine — a 1–2s delay is acceptable. I choose AP (Cassandra) here. For the payment ledger, strong consistency is non-negotiable — I choose CP (Postgres with ACID transactions) there."

Deep Dive Checklist — Use in Every Interview

  • Name the bottleneck before solving it — "The timeline read at 350K RPS is our bottleneck — the DB can't handle this without help."
  • Reference Step 2 numbers — "We estimated 350K RPS in capacity estimation. With 95% cache hit rate, only 17K reach the DB — within Postgres limits."
  • Justify with specifics — "I chose Redis sorted sets because ZRANGEBYSCORE gives us O(log N + M) timeline queries — optimal for cursor pagination."
  • State tradeoffs explicitly — "Fan-out on write adds write latency for celebrities but makes reads O(1). I'm accepting that tradeoff because reads are 100× more frequent."
  • Cover failure modes — "If the cache cluster loses a node, consistent hashing means only 1/N keys remap — the rest are unaffected. Cache miss spike is manageable."
  • Close strongly — "To summarise: the system handles 300M DAU through a cache-first architecture with hybrid fan-out. What area would you like to explore deeper?"

  • Name the bottleneck before proposing the solution
  • State every tradeoff explicitly — "X is better for reads but costs Y on writes"
  • Ask which component the interviewer wants deeper
  • Use real product examples (Cassandra, Redis, Kafka)
  • Cover at least one failure mode per critical component

Don't

  • Jump to solutions without naming the problem first
  • Say "I'd use microservices" without justifying when/why
  • Use Kafka as the answer to every problem
  • Skip consistency/availability trade-offs
  • Forget to mention monitoring/alerting for critical paths
  • Rush through — depth here wins senior interviews
